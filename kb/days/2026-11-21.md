### [The Commodore 64 hardware simulator: break chips and pins, see the results](https://github.com/antirez/c64-hw-simulator) — 6/10

If you repair vintage boards, this lets you reproduce a specific fault, lifting A7 on the color RAM, shorting a pin to +5V, pulling a chip, and read the resulting screen garbage before you desolder anything, so you touch the fewest chips in the most logical order.

Notes: Simulates the Commodore 64 ASSY 250407 motherboard pin by pin from bwack's KiCad replica netlist: 216 parts, their pads, and 249 nets (converted by tools/kicad2c.py). · Cycle-accurate 6510/VIC-II/CIA/SID cores come from floooh's chips library (via c64-llm, zlib); antirez added pin-level models for the DRAMs, ROMs, color RAM, glue logic, and the PLA with its real 82S100 equations (read out by Mark Smith and Marko Mäkelä). · Not an emulator for play: it runs at 31% of real C64 speed on an Apple M3 Max, 39% on a Ryzen AI Max+ PRO 495, and 48% on an Apple M5 Max, because every pin of every chip is simulated. Each half cycle settles in three steps: row address, /RAS falls, /CAS falls. · Electrical model: strongest drive wins a net, equal fighting drives show contention (low wins, magenta on the probe), undriven nets hold charge then drift to ~1.4V with TTL loads or toward 0V with MOS loads. A chip without GND floats its outputs high; a CMOS chip keeps running on phantom power through its pins' protection diodes. Noise is seeded so runs are reproducible. · Scriptable: CLI fault syntax REF:what or REF.PIN:what (U17:removed, U21.14:gnd, R51:open), --headless with --text reading the screen as a person would plus --shot PNG, timed --at faults, and tests/faults.sh covers 31 common faults whose expected symptoms were written down before the simulator ran. Author warns a matching symptom is a clue, not proof. · Stated limits: floating/power thresholds, drift times, and crosstalk are hand-tuned guesses in src/sim.c; video output, SID filter, PSU, and dot-clock PLL are not simulated; in-chip faults (bad DRAM cells, slow parts) and net-to-net bridges are planned but not implemented.

### [Switch Lowering in GCC](https://xoranth.net/gcc-switch) — 6/10

If you have ever profiled a hot dispatch loop and wondered why GCC emitted branches instead of a jump table, this reverse-engineers the exact thresholds that decide it, down to a ratio of 8 at -O3 versus 2 at -Os.

Notes: The jump-table heuristic compares jump-table entries against comparisons, not raw case labels: GCC merges contiguous case ranges first, so a 20-label switch spanning 0-87 (88 entries) lowers to comparisons because it reduces to 10 comparisons and 10*8 < 88 fails the ratio test. · The ratio is set by optimization goal: 8 entries-per-comparison when optimizing for speed (-O1/2/3/fast), 2 when optimizing for size (-Os). Both bitset and jump-table lowering use dynamic programming to minimize the number of clusters. · For small, non-contiguous value sets GCC emits a bitset plus the x86 bt (bit test) instruction against a constant (e.g. 2753205), where set bits are the ASCII case values each offset by 48; the author notes bitset lowering does not transfer to Clang. · GCC detects dense subsets inside sparse switches: an outlier like case 100 gets its own cmp/branch while cases 1-5 still get a jump table. Jump tables below case_values_threshold (defaults to 5 on modern x64/Arm64) are pruned as unprofitable. · Missed optimization flagged by the author: in a sparse-but-regular switch (sparse_8), GCC does not divide x by 8 before indexing, emitting a comparison tree instead. The fallback tree is built by splitting labels into equal-probability halves, using PGO or __builtin_expect_with_probability. · The work is attributed to the pass_lower_switch pass calling analyze_switch_statement, observed under GCC -O3 from disassembly; findings are the author's reverse-engineering, not GCC documentation.

### [MACaQ: Fast, Model Agnostic Calibrated Quantization for Low-Precision Weights and Activations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10649) — 6/10

If the self-reported numbers hold, you can quantize a 122B-parameter model to 4-bit weights and activations in 64 minutes on a single 32.5GB GPU, with only 2.3% inference overhead over plain MXFP4.

Notes: MACaQ is an MXFP4 W4A4 post-training quantization method that treats every linear layer independently, avoiding the cross-layer basis-transform propagation that other recent methods require. · Each layer gets a per-channel scale and a block-Hadamard transform applied to activations online at inference; the inverse transforms are folded into the weights. Keeping transforms online removes cross-layer dependencies and permits a streaming implementation holding only one layer in memory at a time. · Flip-Refinement is a Hessian-aware coordinate-descent step that greedily moves individual FP4 codes to adjacent representable values, improving over round-to-nearest. · Reported results: 2.3% end-to-end overhead over pure MXFP4 on Qwen3-32B in vLLM; lower perplexity than DuQuant++ and MR-GPTQ, matching their best perplexity on Llama-3-8B in 11x less quantization time; a 122B model quantized in 64 minutes using 32.5GB GPU RAM. · The same layer-local design is applied to an image diffusion model without architecture-specific changes, which the authors offer as evidence of generality. · All numbers are author-reported from the arXiv abstract; no independent evaluation, methodology detail, or baseline configuration is provided in the excerpt.
