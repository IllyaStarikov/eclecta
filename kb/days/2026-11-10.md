### [E-means: 10x faster k-means at equal loss](https://e-means.github.io/) — 6/10

If you build vector quantizers, codebooks, or any large-k clustering pipeline, this drops in via faiss/contrib/e_means and claims Lloyd's 25-epoch quality in 1.2 to 3 epochs with no hyperparameters to tune.

Notes: Core change: mini-batch centroid updates use an exponential moving average (forget rate alpha), the same rule VQ-VAE codebook training uses for moving distributions, applied here to static datasets. Lloyd (full-batch recompute) and MacQueen (cumulative mean) are the baselines; Lloyd is a special case of e-means at B=n, alpha=1, which the hyperparameter search finds is a far-from-optimal corner. · Self-reported speedups: 10-45x wall-clock on GPU vs Flash-KMeans, 8-15x on CPU vs Faiss, reaching below Lloyd's 25-epoch test loss in 1.2-3 epochs. All numbers are from the authors' own runs; no independent methodology is given on the page. · No added hyperparameters vs Lloyd: two fitted scaling laws set batch size B and step alpha0 from (n, k, T). Reported regret vs tuned values is under 0.2% on held-out datasets and held-out sizes, with R-squared 0.95-0.96. A 'quality law' trades throughput for lower loss against the 'default' law. · Dead-cluster fix: instead of EnCodec-style resampling from the batch, revive dead clusters by splitting an active cluster chosen with probability proportional to its size (adapted from Faiss). On DEEP, dead clusters fall from 2,343 (14.3%) with resampling to 47 (0.3%); on SIFT, 750 (4.6%) to 10 (0.1%), with lower test MSE. · Provenance and rationale: project builds on Meta FAIR work by Hugo Touvron and Herve Jegou showing gradient-descent mini-batch k-means can beat Lloyd. The author found GD unstable at large learning rates and switched to EMA, arguing EMA keeps each centroid inside the convex hull of its assigned points while GD can overshoot. Mini-batch noise is also credited with escaping local minima that Lloyd exploits (tested over 200 seeds on toy data). · Validation scope: 24 embedding/synthetic datasets and configurations in the paper; the results video covers 234 e-means-vs-k-means runs across 16 datasets, 100k to 100M points. Code lives in faiss/contrib/e_means; paper is arXiv:2611.09501.

### [Horible public key tricks](https://maurycyz.com/projects/badcert) — 6/10

A CA will sign a certificate whose RSA modulus spells out a human-readable message, so the "random" public key in a trusted cert is attacker-controllable to the bit and worth remembering next time you reason about what a CA actually validates.

Notes: Method: pick two random primes, compute n, overwrite bytes in n's upper half to embed an ASCII message in its base64 encoding, then restore a valid factorization by setting q to the next prime after n/p. Because p and q are close in size, incrementing q only perturbs the lower half of the product, leaving the message in the top half intact. · The CA (Let's Encrypt, per the pasted cert) refuses non-standard public exponents, so e stays 65537 (0x10001); only the modulus is used as the message carrier. The author embedded text including 'No one checks these things' and a jab at a CA that 'got away with the number 4 for a year.' · Capacity is roughly 150 characters for RSA-2048 and 320 for RSA-4096 in base64; targeting the hex view browsers show would roughly double density (2 chars/byte vs 0.75) but constrains word choice. Base64's 3-byte grouping can misalign the message, fixable in under 4 placement attempts since the cert layout is static. · Self-reported security caveats: the technique lowers private-key entropy; the script does not check for insecure outputs such as p and q too close together (enabling Fermat-style factoring) or a very small d. Author argues the cert is still fine for a public site because modern TLS forward secrecy means a leaked key alone does not enable passive decryption, only active MITM. · Framed as a 'protest certificate' against the ACME/expiry treadmill rather than a real alternative to PKI; author concedes such modulus-fixing tricks were studied as RSA compression (fixing up to 2/3 of n for 1/3 size) but largely abandoned for cryptosystems that natively use small keys.
