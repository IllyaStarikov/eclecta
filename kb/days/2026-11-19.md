Notes: recovery_prefetch converts the primary's large sequential I/O into 8k random reads on replicas, which the author measures as up to 16x IOPS amplification for the same bytes read; the repro shows a replica pinned at its 500 IOPS disk limit for 40 minutes while the primary ran at roughly 300 IOPS. · The outage mechanism combines two things: synchronous replication (primary blocks all writes until a standby confirms) and the standby state machine in src/backend/access/transam/xlogrecovery.c, where a standby that drops into WAL-restore mode will not resume streaming until it has fully caught up. · Repro recipe (published at github.com/ardentperf-labs/walreceiver-outage-repro): a 2-CPU, 8GB, 500-IOPS cloud instance, pgbench scale 640 (~10GB), update 5% of pgbench_accounts to create a write burst, then kill the walreceiver to simulate a network glitch; TPS drops to zero and writes hang 30+ minutes, longer with bigger tables. · The concern was raised on postgres-hackers in Feb 2021 by Stephen Frost (before the feature shipped ~18 months later) and dismissed; Michael Zhilin of Postgres Pro reported a real customer case in Mar 2025 of multi-hour replay lag, noting the prefetch code issues fadvise calls even when a block is served from a WAL full-page image (FPI) rather than disk. · The author's own finding beyond prior reports is the sequential-to-random IOPS amplification; disabling recovery_prefetch (with FPWs still on) eliminated the post-update stall and let the standby apply the 5% update in under 10 minutes. · Recommendations are the author's lean, not settled: disable recovery_prefetch across the board (he says its benefit is dubious with FPWs on by default) and consider three replicas with quorum commit ANY 1; he states he wants to see where the hackers discussion goes.

### [[$] Beyond the &](https://lwn.net/Articles/1096028) — 6/10

If this lands, wrapping a struct in a MutexGuard or custom smart pointer stops breaking disjoint-field borrows, removing a class of `&mut *guard` reborrow workarounds that trip up Rust code today.

Notes: The failing case: `state.cache.entry(...).or_insert_with(|| ...state.template...)` compiles through a `&mut RenderState` because the borrow checker tracks `cache` and `template` as disjoint fields, but fails through a `MutexGuard<RenderState>`, which it sees as an opaque access that might alias. Current workaround is to reborrow via `let state: &mut RenderState = &mut *state.lock().unwrap();`. · Mechanism: expose the compiler's internal 'place' concept (Rust's lvalue, an abstract compile-time location like `state.cache`) to user code. Each smart pointer gets a handle type, often a wrapper around an unsafe pointer, and the compiler auto-creates handles for referenced places. · Four named traits carry the semantics: ReadPlace, WritePlace, ProjectPlace (turn a place for a struct into a place for one of its fields, e.g. `MutexGuard<RenderState>` to `MutexGuard<String>`), and BorrowPlace (create a new borrowing smart pointer, the role `&` plays for built-ins). Mandry cited six to eight operations total per handle, including a VariantPlace trait for destructive pattern matching. · Trait impls are `unsafe` because a wrong impl can make the borrow checker unsound; `WritePlace::SAFE = false` tells the checker to treat writes as unsafe. The same mechanism can redefine built-in behavior: a `RefHandle` impl of `BorrowPlace<&'a T>` sets `ACCESS = AccessKind::Shared`, `Timing = Lifetime<'a>`, `SAFE = true`. · Status and caveats: still a prototype, not stable; Mandry is requesting weird-smart-pointer examples against the design docs. Open questions include borrow syntax (`&` risks confusing type inference, proposed `@` is also unpopular) and diagnostics (the new traits should stay hidden from error messages). Side benefits claimed: pattern matching through smart pointers, and possibly field projection through enums like `Option` (left unresolved).
