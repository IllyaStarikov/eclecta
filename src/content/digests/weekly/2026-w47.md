## Reasoning effort, priced and weighed

The week's most practical result is a negative one. Dex Horthy's [October 2026 SlopCodeBench run](https://github.com/humanlayer/advanced-context-engineering-for-coding-agents/blob/main/benchmarking-new-models-on-slop-code-bench.md) put ten frontier model-and-effort campaigns through a staged test: an agent builds a program, gets new requirements, and must keep old behavior working across 36 problems and 196 checkpoints. Turning reasoning effort from Medium up to XHigh cost 2.11x to 4.22x more and raised the strict pass rate for only one model. On Opus 5.5 it fell, from 28 strict passes to 25 at 4.22x the cost; on GPT-6 SOL it fell from 30 to 26 at 2.11x; only GPT-6 Astra improved, 29 to 32. Horthy stresses these are single runs that cannot separate an effort effect from run-to-run variance, which is the honest caveat that makes the result worth reading rather than discarding.

The absolute numbers set the scene. Strict passes (every test category green, including regression, with no earlier checkpoint broken) clustered at 12.2% to 16.3%, led by GPT-6 Astra XHigh at 16.33% (32/196). The looser core rate (main behavior in a nonempty category passes) ran 65.8% to 74.0%. The gap is the finding: models get the main job right far more often than they keep everything working as requirements change. Cost tracked nothing useful, spanning $178.51 for GPT-6.1 SOL Medium (same strict count as Astra Medium) to $1,550.07 for Fable 5.1 XHigh, which scored three strict passes fewer. A separate slop metric, the share of source lines flagged by 200-plus linters, cut against intuition too: Opus Medium scored cleaner on strict passes than Opus XHigh yet flagged 94.25% of lines versus XHigh's 77.75%. Horthy notes the linter battery was curated from pre-March-2026 agent PRs and may miss newer anti-patterns. A qualitative Part II from Gabe Orlanski is pending.

## Agents post-training a model, measured

The complementary story is a positive claim, and a bolder one. [Athanor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21055), an open "agent-native" system, had four Claude Opus 5.5 agents, each on an eight-GPU node and given no human research advice, spend 104 hours post-training a raw Qwen3-4B base model using only public data. The authors report it went from 15.9 to 67.3 on a six-task development suite and from 9.2 to 48.2 on a held-out suite the agents did not know existed; the official Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 scores 66.5 and 47.9 on those suites. The agents recombined known methods rather than inventing any: supervised fine-tuning on public reasoning data, RL with a rollout tweak one agent introduced, cross-agent weight merges, and rounds of self-distillation.

The framing is deliberately modest: a weak, testable form of recursive self-improvement, frontier agents training a small model as a measure of research potential, not a path to a frontier-scale system. The limits are larger than the abstract admits. Every number is self-reported, the six-task suite composition is not disclosed, and no independent replication exists. Read as a claim it is a significant one: that autonomous post-training now produces a checkable artifact rather than a thought experiment. It is not yet a confirmed result.

## Old infrastructure, new failure modes

A Postgres setting that shipped around 2022 can [hang all writes for 30 minutes to hours](https://ardentperf.com/2026/10/20/postgres-recovery_prefetch-considered-harmful-and-causing-outages) on the common production configuration: synchronous replication plus full-page writes. The author's new finding is that `recovery_prefetch` converts the primary's large sequential I/O into 8k random reads on replicas, amplifying read IOPS by as much as 16x for the same bytes and exhausting cheap disks. Because synchronous replication blocks the primary until a standby confirms, and the standby state machine in `xlogrecovery.c` will not resume streaming until it fully catches up, a write burst coinciding with a network glitch stalls everything. A published pgbench repro on a 500-IOPS cloud instance triggers the hang by killing the walreceiver after updating 5% of a table; disabling `recovery_prefetch`, with full-page writes still on, let the standby apply the same update in under 10 minutes.

The history matters for how operators should weigh it. Stephen Frost raised the concern on postgres-hackers in February 2021, before the feature shipped, and it was dismissed; Postgres Pro's Michael Zhilin reported a real multi-hour replay-lag case in March 2025, noting the code issues fadvise calls even when a block is served from a WAL full-page image rather than disk. The recommendation to disable the setting broadly is the author's lean, not settled upstream policy; he says he wants to see where the hackers discussion goes. For anyone running synchronous Postgres, it is one setting worth auditing before the next network blip does it for you.

## Quick hits

- AWS published [its own local emulator](https://github.com/aws/aws-emulator) of the AWS APIs, written in Rust with one crate per service driven by a Smithy model, serving HTTPS on 127.0.0.1:9297 and offering an embedded mode that injects its HTTP client straight into the SDK. Per-service fidelity ships as published scores; AWS says not to use it for production or security testing, and the repo rejects pull requests. Building from source needs a Rust toolchain, a C compiler, and at least 100 GB of disk, since every service is its own crate.
